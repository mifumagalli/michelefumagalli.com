Galaxies do not form stars in isolation. They pull gas in from their
surroundings and blow it back out again, and the balance between those two
flows is what ultimately decides how many stars get made. The difficulty is
that the gas doing the deciding is nearly invisible — too diffuse to see
directly, detectable mostly as shadows it casts on light passing through it.

There are two ways to catch those shadows, and they answer different
questions. Look straight at a galaxy and you see gas moving toward you,
silhouetted against the galaxy's own light: that is material being expelled.
Look instead at a background object whose light happens to pass near a
foreground galaxy, and you probe the gas out at some distance from it. The
first tells you about the wind at launch; the second, where it ends up.

The MUDF survey is deep enough to do both in the same field — 84 galaxies
observed down the barrel, 360 pairs in the transverse direction, reaching
galaxies as small as a hundred million solar masses.

What emerges is a clean mass dependence, and it runs in two directions at
once. Massive galaxies show the strongest absorption — they hold on to more
cool gas — but their outflows are slow. Low-mass galaxies show weaker
absorption and faster winds. Their shallower gravity lets gas leave more
easily, and at higher speed.

The second result is a caution. Standard models of an expanding gas halo
predict more absorption than we actually measure, and the transverse profile
falls off more gently with distance than those models expect. The most likely
explanation is that real outflows are not spherical shells but narrow cones,
covering only around a seventh of the sky as seen from the galaxy.

Mass sets how much cool gas a galaxy keeps. But the shape of the outflow
decides how much of it we ever see.
