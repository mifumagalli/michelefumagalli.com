We recently observed a single filament of the cosmic web, stretched
between two quasars in the MUSE Ultra Deep Field. One filament is a proof of
principle. The obvious next question is whether such structures are rare
exceptions, lit up by unusual neighbours, or the normal state of affairs
wherever galaxies gather.

To find out, we combined two of the deepest observations ever taken with
MUSE, the MUSE Ultra Deep Field and the MUSE Extremely Deep Field, more than
250 hours of exposure between them, and analysed both in exactly the same
way. Instead of following quasars, we followed ordinary galaxies: young,
star-forming objects that shine via hydrogen emission.

We identified 41 such crowded regions between redshift 3 and 5, when the
universe was one to two billion years old. They hold two to ten times more
galaxies than average. In about three quarters of them we see faint,
extended emission reaching beyond the gas that belongs to
individual galaxies: filaments of the web itself, usually lined up with the
direction traced by the galaxies.

The environment matters. In the least crowded regions we mostly see nothing.
In intermediate regions the brightness varies by about a factor of ten from
one structure to the next. In the most crowded regions the emission levels
off.

The glowing patches do not fill the filaments. They cover only 20–25 per cent
of the area, and the rate at which a random line of sight would cross one
matches how often quasar spectra run into Lyman limit systems, the clumps of
partially neutral gas that absorption studies have catalogued for decades.
That points to what we are actually seeing: the denser spines of filaments
and the substructures inside them, with roughly 0.001 to 0.1 hydrogen atoms
per cubic centimetre.

Fields this deep will always be few. But stacking many shallower
observations, each aligned along its galaxy distribution, should extend this
picture to hundreds of structures before the next generation of
wide-field spectrographs arrives.
